# Related literacies

**Navigating today’s information-rich world requires critical thinking and the ability to work with digital technologies and data. Besides data literacy, other relevant and overlapping literacies are digital literacy and information literacy.**

Let's start with two definitions. First, *Digital* literacy can be defined as the ability to navigate ethically and professionally in today’s digital world, as well as to develop a critical mindset and staying up-to-date with digital technologies. This also encompasses aspects of AI. Secondly, *Information* literacy can be defined as the ability to find, access, evaluate, (re)use and create (scientific) information. An information literate individual knows to avoid dependency on common search engines or AI-powered searches.

At the conceptual level, questions still arise when considering the two definitions above along the one for [data literacy](../about/what-is-data-literacy.md). For instance, what is the different between *data* and *information*? Or, when analysing data with AI-powered tools, is one dealing mostly with data or digital literacy? And can it be both?

Staying at such a conceptual level can be too abstract. So let's take it step-by-step until we get to some practical examples.

## Academic Skills map
Going back to a more abstract level, we can focus on specific *processes*,  actions, or topics that academic work typically entails or addresses. The figure below aims to illustrate how some steps overlap and intersect across literacies. For example, both information and data literacy require some sort of *plan* in order get the best results. "Reusing" literature might seem like a strange term, but technically that is what we do when (re)reading publications with refined knowledge or fresh prespectives. Reusing data is in principle similar, but it is still a rare practice in some disciplines. Among other things, it could be argued this is because the data literacy of some researchers has not allowed them to facilitate the *reusability* of their collected data.

Topics like "Ethics & Integrity", "Privacy & Security", or "Copyright" are also present across literacies. For example, in the challenge of collecting and analysing personally sensitive data or information. In contrast, a topic like "Well-being" can be more easily related to digital literacy, in relation to the (mis)use of novel technologies like AI.

Naturally, despite what the figure below shows, navigating the landscape is rarely so straightforward, but having a sense of direction despite such a complexity is a sign of literacy!
