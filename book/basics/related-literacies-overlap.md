# Related literacies

**Navigating today’s information-rich world requires critical thinking and the ability to work with digital technologies and data. Besides data literacy, other relevant and overlapping literacies are *digital* literacy and *information* literacy.**

Let's start with two definitions. First, *Digital* literacy can be defined as the ability to navigate ethically and professionally in today’s digital world, as well as to develop a critical mindset and staying up-to-date with digital technologies. This also encompasses aspects of AI. Secondly, *Information* literacy can be defined as the ability to find, access, evaluate, (re)use and create (scientific) information. An information literate individual knows to avoid dependency on common search engines or AI-powered searches.

At such a conceptual level, you can expect that challenging questions still arise or remain when considering the two definitions above along the one for [data literacy](../about/what-is-data-literacy.md). For instance:

- what is the difference between *data* and *information*? or,
- when analysing data with AI-powered tools, is one dealing mostly with data or digital literacy? And can it be both?

Staying at such a conceptual level can be too abstract when discussing multiple literacies. So let's break this down step-by-step until we get to some practical examples.

## Academic Skills map
Returning to a more abstract level, you can relate multiple literacies by  specific *steps* or *topics* that academic work typically entails or addresses.

The figure below aims to illustrate how some steps and topics overlap and intersect across literacies. For example, a best practice in both information and data literacy is to have some sort of *plan* in order to be efficient and potentially also to get the best results.

<img width="1000" height="585" alt="Metromap literacies" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/38764492-70bf-46bb-bfc4-0d1e632a701d"/><br>

 The map is indicative but *not* exhaustive or comprehensive of all overlapping steps or topics. For example, "reusing" literature might seem like a strange term, but technically that is what you do when (re)reading publications with refined knowledge or fresh prespectives. Reusing data is in principle a similar step, but it is still rarely practiced in some disciplines. In this case, it could be argued that the data literacy of some researchers has not allowed them to facilitate the *reusability* of their collected data.

Topics like "Ethics & Integrity", "Privacy & Security", or "Copyright" are also very important across literacies. For example, in the challenge of collecting and analysing personally sensitive data or information. In contrast, a topic like "Well-being" can be more easily related to digital literacy, in relation to the (mis)use of novel technologies like AI.

Finally, keep in mind that, despite what the map shows, navigating the landscape is rarely so straightforward. Tackling each step or topic can demand a lot of time and/or resources. Nevertheless, having a sense of direction while navigating throughout such complexity is a sign of literacy!
